Chapter Four

Elior
the Listener

Listening becomes instinct, exposure, restraint, and a quieter form of courage.

Elior became known, without intending to, as someone who listened.

People noticed it first in small ways. He did not interrupt. He did not hurry responses into place. When spoken to, he allowed a pause before answering, as though conversation required patience before return. Some mistook this for uncertainty. Others sensed something steadier beneath it, though they rarely attempted to name it.

For Elior, listening was not a discipline. It was instinct.

Words revealed themselves slowly when given room. When pressed, they collapsed into habit, borrowing confidence they had not earned. Silence, by contrast, allowed thought to gather its own weight before taking form.

In the city, this tendency set him apart without his choosing.

Prayer here was meant to be spoken aloud. Listening during prayer was permitted but rarely emphasized. Faith moved outward. Statement followed statement. Listening was treated as pause rather than substance.

Elior sensed the imbalance keenly.

He often stood at the edges of gatherings, hearing prayers spill into the open air. He listened not only to what was spoken, but to how it was carried. He noticed the slight tightening before certain phrases, the lift of the voice at expected moments, the urgency concealed beneath measured calm.

He heard where confidence practiced itself.

He did not judge this. He recognized it.

On more than one occasion, someone asked him to pray aloud. The request was offered politely, almost kindly, as though inviting him to share what he carried so quietly.

Elior never refused directly. When he prayed, his words came slowly, separated by pauses that unsettled some listeners. He did not disguise the silence or rush toward conclusion.

Afterward, people thanked him with courtesy, their tone suggesting that something had not entirely arrived.

He noticed.

Each time, he returned to listening.

At home, when alone, prayer proved more difficult.

Without witnesses, the impulse to speak faltered. Words surfaced, then dissolved. His attention turned inward, away from phrasing and toward sensation, breath, tension, and thought moving without definition.

He waited for language to take hold.

Often, it did not.

Gradually, he recognized that prayer, for him, did not begin in speech. It began in awareness, in the unsettling intimacy of remembered presence. Listening did not soften this experience. It removed the reassurance of performance.

At times, Elior wondered whether he was avoiding commitment.

Speaking required exposure. To pray aloud was to stand visible. Silence, by contrast, could be mistaken for neutrality. He wrestled with the fear that his listening served as a way of withholding himself.

The possibility troubled him.

One evening, alone at home, he tested it.

He stood to pray, determined to speak through discomfort. Words arrived, tentative but sincere. As he continued, the familiar distance emerged, the sense that his speech was advancing ahead of his belief.

He stopped.

Listening returned.

He sat down slowly, frustration tightening his chest. He had sought courage and encountered restraint instead. Whether one surpassed the other, he could not say.

Outside, the city continued speaking.

Prayers rose and fell through walls and open windows. He did not resent them. He felt instead a quiet grief that something essential might be slipping past within all that sound.

Late that night, Elior walked briefly through the streets.

Nearby, a man prayed loudly, pacing with restless energy, words spilling freely. A few passersby paused to listen, visibly reassured. The prayer pressed forward insistently, as though testing resistance in the air.

Elior observed from a distance.

He noticed how rarely the man paused, how each phrase reinforced the next. The prayer did not hesitate. It did not weaken.

When it ended, the man exhaled deeply, satisfied.

Elior felt something different.

He sensed that prayer offered in this way left little room for listening, not for God, and not for oneself. Everything was occupied. Everything was filled.

Was listening faith, or avoidance?

The question followed him as he turned for home.

He passed the narrow corridor beside his house, a passage he rarely used. The door stood slightly open. He paused without understanding why.

Inside, the air felt still.

He did not enter.

Not yet.

He closed his own door behind him and remained standing for a moment in the quiet of his home, aware of language hovering just beneath acknowledgment.

Listening, he realized, required a distinct kind of courage.

It asked him to remain present without resolution.

He went to sleep carrying that tension with him, unrelieved and intact.
